\section{The big picture}
\label{sec:bigpicture}

The goal is simple to state: know where a small rover is inside a room, to within a
few centimetres, many times per second --- and know which way it is pointing.

The tool is ultra-wideband (UWB) radio. Five small radio stations, called
\textbf{anchors}, are fixed around the room at known positions. One or two radios
called \textbf{tags} ride on the rover. A tag can measure its distance to an anchor
by timing how long a radio message takes to fly there and back. Radio moves at the
speed of light --- about 30\,cm every nanosecond --- so to get centimetres the
timing must be good to a few tens of \emph{picoseconds}. The DW1000 radio chip is
built for exactly this.

\begin{plainwords}
Think of each distance measurement as a piece of string from an anchor to the rover.
One string tells you the rover is somewhere on a circle. Two strings narrow it to two
points. Three or more pin it down. Because every string has a little error, the
strings never agree perfectly, so we look for the point that keeps \emph{all} of them
as close to happy as possible. Everything else in this document --- calibration,
filtering, two-tag geometry --- exists to make the strings more truthful and the
disagreement-settling smarter.
\end{plainwords}

The data flows through five stages, each covered by a part of this document:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Ranging} (Part~III). The tag performs a timed message exchange with
      each anchor in turn and reports the distances over USB or WiFi.
\item \textbf{Correction} (Part~III). Each raw distance is corrected for two known
      instrument errors: the antenna's internal delay, and a bias that depends on
      signal strength.
\item \textbf{Solving} (Part~IV). Corrected distances become a position by weighted
      least squares.
\item \textbf{Filtering} (Part~IV). Positions are smoothed over time by a Kalman
      filter or a moving-horizon estimator, which know that rovers move smoothly.
\item \textbf{Rigid-body fusion} (Part~V). With two tags bolted to one plate, we
      estimate the rover's whole pose --- centre, heading, speed --- in one solve.
\end{enumerate}

Independently of all that, a Kinect camera on the ceiling watches a printed AprilTag
marker on the rover. After careful calibration (Part~II) the camera is trusted as
\textbf{ground truth}: it tells us where the rover \emph{really} is, so we can
honestly score how well the radio system does.

\section{Calibrating the Kinect until it can be trusted}
\label{sec:camera}

Before believing any radio measurement, we needed an independent instrument that is
\emph{more} accurate than the thing being tested. A downward-looking camera can be
that instrument, but only after calibration. This section is the story of that
calibration, including two wrong answers on the way --- kept here deliberately,
because the \emph{method} of catching the wrong answers is the useful part.

\subsection{The geometry of looking straight down}

For a camera at height $h$ looking down, a flat object on the floor appears with a
size proportional to $f/h$, where $f$ is the focal length in pixels. One pixel on
the sensor corresponds to $h/f$ metres on the floor. With $h \approx 4$\,m and
$f \approx 1068$\,px, one pixel is about 3.8\,mm of floor. Sub-centimetre truth is
therefore possible --- \emph{if} $h$ and $f$ are right. Get $h$ wrong by 5\,\% and
every distance the camera reports is wrong by 5\,\%.

\subsection{Measuring the height by parallax}
\label{sec:parallax}

You cannot hang a tape measure from the ceiling and call it done --- the optical
centre of the camera is somewhere inside the housing. Instead we measured $h$ with
the camera itself, using \textbf{parallax}: raise an object by a known amount and
watch how far its image shifts.

Consider a point on the floor at radial image distance $\rho_1$ from the point
directly beneath the camera (the \emph{nadir}). Raise it by $\Delta z$ (we used a
box of measured thickness 0.355\,m) and it appears further out, at $\rho_2$,
because it is now closer to the camera. Similar triangles give
\begin{equation}
\frac{\rho_2}{\rho_1} \;=\; \frac{h}{\,h-\Delta z\,}
\qquad\Longrightarrow\qquad
h \;=\; \Delta z \,\frac{\rho_2}{\,\rho_2-\rho_1\,}.
\end{equation}

\begin{plainwords}
Hold your thumb at arm's length and raise it towards your eye: it drifts away from
the centre of your view. How \emph{fast} it drifts depends on how far away your eye
is. The camera's height falls out of the same effect, measured in pixels.
\end{plainwords}

\begin{pitfall}
Our first height estimate, 3.873\,m, came from a single sloppy session and was
simply wrong. Two careful repeats (box hops in all four quadrants of the image,
plumb-bob to transfer the floor point) both said $\approx$4.21\,m --- reproducible,
so we almost believed it. It was \emph{also} wrong, by about 3\,\%: uncorrected
image distortion biases raw-pixel parallax high. The final method
(\S\ref{sec:stagec}) works in floor coordinates instead of raw pixels and gave
\textbf{4.066\,m} with a 7.7\,mm fit residual. The lesson: reproducibility is
necessary but not sufficient --- a systematic bias reproduces beautifully.
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Focal length, and the consistency test that settled everything}
\label{sec:stagec}

The refined procedure (implemented in
\gh{matlab/vision/calibrateOverheadCamera.m}{calibrateOverheadCamera.m} and
friends) solves for height and focal length together, using box hops expressed in
metric floor coordinates through a fitted floor homography. It returned
$h = 4.066$\,m and $f = 1067.6$\,px.

The number was accepted only after an \emph{independent} cross-check. The ratio
$h/f$ is metres-per-pixel on the floor, and we can measure that directly by laying
tape measures in the image (\gh{matlab/vision/addScaleBars.m}{addScaleBars.m}).
The two agreed to 0.2\,\%:
\[
h/f = 4.066/1067.6 = 3.808\,\text{mm/px}
\qquad\text{vs}\qquad
3.815\,\text{mm/px from tape}.
\]
When two genuinely independent methods agree that closely, the calibration is done.

\subsection{Lens distortion: measured, then deliberately ignored}

Wide lenses bend straight lines. We photographed stretched strings across the field
and measured the bow: \textbf{under 1.5 pixels everywhere} --- about 6\,mm of floor,
at the edge of the field, worst case. We then tried fitting a distortion model
anyway, and it confidently produced a distortion centre far from the image centre:
it was fitting our mouse-click noise, not the lens. The model was discarded and the
lens declared straight. Knowing when \emph{not} to model something is calibration
skill too.

\subsection{Tying the camera to the room, and clicking the anchors in}
\label{sec:registration}

The camera reports positions in its own frame. To connect it to the room we placed
the AprilTag at several positions, clicked its corners, and solved the rigid
transform camera$\to$world
(\gh{matlab/vision/registerWorldFrameClicks.m}{registerWorldFrameClicks.m}).
Registration residual: \textbf{13.5\,mm RMS}, worst point 18.7\,mm.

\begin{pitfall}
The printed AprilTag measures 17.78\,cm, but the detector behaves as if it were
\textbf{16.9\,cm} --- a real property of how the detector defines a tag's border,
not a printing error. Until this was anchored against tape measurements, every
camera distance carried a hidden 5\,\% scale error. If you use AprilTags for
metrology, calibrate the \emph{effective} size, not the printed one.
\end{pitfall}

With registration done, the camera became a point-measuring machine: click any
pixel whose height above the floor is known, get room coordinates
(\gh{matlab/vision/clickTagTruth.m}{clickTagTruth.m}). The five anchors were
positioned exactly this way --- clicked, not taped
(\gh{matlab/vision/clickAnchorsWorld.m}{clickAnchorsWorld.m}) --- which is why the
anchor file's layout is named \code{clicked\_kinect}.

\begin{tryit}
To measure a parked tag's true position (used throughout calibration):
\begin{lstlisting}
truth = clickTagTruth(0.24, struct('uwbTagIds', 240));
% 0.24 = the tag antenna's height above the floor in metres.
% Click the antenna in the snapshot; world (x,y) comes back.
\end{lstlisting}
The height argument matters: a click is a ray from the camera, and the height picks
the point along that ray. An error of $\Delta z$ shifts the answer by roughly
$\rho\,\Delta z/h$ --- at 2\,m from the nadir, 1\,cm of height error is
$\sim$5\,mm of position error.
\end{tryit}

\section{Two-way ranging}
\label{sec:twr}

\subsection{The idea, and why it takes four timestamps}

A tag cannot simply timestamp a message and have the anchor timestamp its arrival:
the two clocks are not synchronised, and never will be to picoseconds. Instead the
tag times a \emph{round trip}, which uses only its own clock. But then the anchor's
processing delay sits inside the measurement, and worse, the two clocks tick at
very slightly different \emph{rates} (crystal oscillators differ by parts per
million --- at nanosecond scales that matters).

The fix is \textbf{double-sided two-way ranging}: both sides measure both a round
trip and a reply delay, and the four numbers are combined as
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:dstwr}
t_{\text{flight}} \;=\;
\frac{T^{\text{tag}}_{\text{round}}\; T^{\text{anc}}_{\text{round}}
      \;-\; T^{\text{tag}}_{\text{reply}}\; T^{\text{anc}}_{\text{reply}}}
     {T^{\text{tag}}_{\text{round}} + T^{\text{anc}}_{\text{round}}
      + T^{\text{tag}}_{\text{reply}} + T^{\text{anc}}_{\text{reply}}},
\qquad d = c\, t_{\text{flight}} .
\end{equation}

\begin{plainwords}
Each side asks: ``how long did I wait in total, and how long did I spend replying?''
Multiplying and dividing the four durations in this particular pattern makes the
unknown clock-rate difference cancel to first order. We specifically audited that
our firmware uses this product form (in
\gh{libraries/UwbRtls/src/TwrEngine.cpp}{TwrEngine.cpp}) --- because if it had used
the simpler averaging form, clock drift would have been a suspect for the drift
problem of \S\ref{sec:breathing}. It is not.
\end{plainwords}

The chip's time unit sets the ruler: one DW1000 tick is
$1/(128 \times 499.2\,\text{MHz}) \approx 15.65$\,ps, which at the speed of light is
\begin{equation}
1\ \text{tick} \;\approx\; \textbf{4.69 mm}.
\end{equation}
This number appears throughout the calibration tools: one antenna-delay tick moves
every measured distance by 4.69\,mm.

\subsection{How a measurement round actually runs}
\label{sec:protocol}

One tag ranges to the five anchors \emph{in turn} --- one exchange at a time, so
radios never talk over each other
(\gh{libraries/UwbRtls/src/UwbScheduler.cpp}{UwbScheduler.cpp}). A full sweep of
five anchors takes roughly 125\,ms, giving the 5--7\,Hz update rate. An anchor that
fails twice in a row is skipped for a few sweeps with exponential back-off, so a
dead anchor cannot stall the ring. With two tags, a token-passing ring
(\gh{libraries/UwbRtls/src/TagRing.cpp}{TagRing.cpp}) alternates sweeps between
them --- which is why each tag reports at $\sim$3\,Hz when both run.

After each sweep the tag prints one text line (and, if WiFi is up, broadcasts the
same line as a UDP packet). The format is deliberately boring --- versioned ASCII,
one line per sweep:
\begin{center}\small
\code{RTLS,v4,<ms>,<tag>,<n>,\{id,mm,rx,fp,q,cfo,tex\}$\times$n,DIAG,temp,vbat[,IMU,...]}
\end{center}
carrying per anchor: distance (mm), received power and first-path power (dBm),
quality, carrier-frequency offset and slot timing (diagnostics added during the
investigation of \S\ref{sec:breathing}); then radio temperature and battery; then
the IMU sample if the tag has one. Parsed by
\gh{matlab/+dune/parseRtlsLine.m}{parseRtlsLine.m}, which accepts both v3 and v4.

\begin{pitfall}
A failed diagnostic read inside the chip yields the sentinel value
\code{-2147483648} in the power fields. Early on this poisoned averages silently.
Every consumer now rejects sentinel-bearing measurements explicitly
(see \code{SENTINEL} in \gh{matlab/+dune/solveSweep.m}{solveSweep.m}).
\end{pitfall}

\subsection{Sharing the air between two tags: two modes}
\label{sec:coord}

Only one radio may transmit at a time, or the exchanges collide. With two tags on
the rover this needs a referee, and the system supports \textbf{two modes}, chosen
at firmware-build time. They differ only in \emph{how the two tags agree whose turn
it is}; everything downstream (the host line format, the solver, the rigid
estimator) is identical.

\textbf{Mode A --- wireless token ring} (default,
\gh{libraries/UwbRtls/src/TagRing.cpp}{TagRing.cpp}). The two tags pass a ``token''
to each other \emph{over the UWB air itself}: whoever holds it sweeps, then hands it
on. No wires. The cost is that the hand-off rides the same lossy shared channel it
is trying to coordinate --- a dropped token frame means a missed turn, and in
practice one tag can end up starved of slots (the first rover run captured 1124
sweeps from the front tag but only 386 from the rear, which weakened the heading).

\textbf{Mode B --- wired master/slave} (\code{UWB\_COORD\_WIRE};
\gh{libraries/UwbRtls/src/TagLink.cpp}{TagLink.cpp}). Because the two tags are
bolted 52.7\,cm apart on the same rigid plate, a short cable between them is trivial
--- so coordination is moved \emph{off} the radio and onto a dedicated wire. One
board is the \textbf{master} (the one with the IMU and WiFi); it runs the schedule
and grants the other board --- the \textbf{slave} --- a slot over the wire. Exactly
one DW1000 is ever active, and whose turn it is is decided by cable, never by a radio
message that can be lost.

\begin{plainwords}
The token ring is like two people in a dark room taking turns to shout, agreeing
whose turn it is \emph{by shouting} --- which fails exactly when the room is noisy.
The wired link is a tap on the shoulder instead: it never gets drowned out. Since the
two tags are rigidly a half-metre apart anyway, running a wire between them costs
almost nothing and removes the coordination traffic from the channel entirely.
\end{plainwords}

The wire runs a UART at 921\,600\,baud with a compact binary protocol: each frame
carries a start byte, a \emph{protocol version} (so mismatched firmware is rejected
at the parser, not silently believed), a type, a length, and a CRC-16 check. The
scheduler is keyed on a cycle number that every message carries, and link-health
counters (CRC errors, resyncs, sequence gaps) make a bad cable visible immediately.
A nice efficiency falls out of the design: while the slave uses its slot, the master
is not idle waiting --- it spends that time on its own IMU read, host send and
display, so the slave's sweep overlaps the master's housekeeping rather than adding
to the cycle.

When the master forwards the slave's measurement to the host it appends a small tail
(\code{,CYC,<cycle>[,MRX,<us>]}) recording the cycle id and the master's own receive
timestamp, so the host can line the two tags' streams up exactly;
\gh{matlab/+dune/parseRtlsLine.m}{parseRtlsLine.m} reads it. The rigid estimator
does not care which mode produced the data --- it already treats each incoming sweep
independently (\S\ref{sec:rigid}) --- so the wired link is a drop-in reliability
upgrade for the two-tag case, aimed squarely at the slot-starvation that hurt the
first run.

\section{Calibrating the radios}
\label{sec:calibration}

Raw DW1000 distances are wrong by metres. Two corrections bring them to centimetres.

\subsection{Antenna delay --- and the trap in stored memory}
\label{sec:delay}

The signal spends time inside each board --- silicon, PCB traces, the antenna
itself --- before it physically leaves. The timer cannot tell this from flight
time, so every board carries a calibration constant (``antenna delay'') subtracted
in hardware. Each tick of it is the 4.69\,mm above.

\begin{pitfall}
The delay constants live in the ESP32's non-volatile storage (NVS), and
\textbf{NVS survives re-flashing}. Boards carried garbage values from months
earlier: one anchor measured $+4$\,m, another $+1$\,m, consistently, with tight
scatter --- it looked exactly like a software bug. The tell was that the errors
were \emph{constant per anchor} and survived every host-side change. A factory
reset of the stored values fixed it instantly. Any future bring-up should wipe NVS
first. (The boot banner now always prints which delay source is active, so this is
visible at every connect.)
\end{pitfall}

Calibration then proceeded against camera truth: park the tag at a clicked
reference point, compare each anchor's median measured distance with the true
distance, convert the error to ticks ($\div 4.69$\,mm), push the adjustment to the
anchor over the air (\code{SETANTDELAY}), repeat. Implemented in
\gh{matlab/scripts/tune\_delays\_sweep.m}{tune\_delays\_sweep.m}; the tag's own
delay is tuned the same way by
\gh{matlab/scripts/tune\_tag\_delay.m}{tune\_tag\_delay.m} (tag 241 converged in a
single step: a $-507$\,mm common offset became $-2$\,mm).

\begin{result}
After two rounds, all five anchors measured within $\pm16$\,mm at the reference
point, and the solved position there landed \textbf{4\,mm} from camera truth.
\end{result}

\subsection{An anomaly we could not explain}
\label{sec:anomaly}

During calibration we found that the \emph{same} ranging routine gives a different
answer depending on how it is called. Rapid back-to-back exchanges (the burst mode
used by a hardware self-calibration command) and the normal round-robin sweep
disagree by a constant per-anchor offset between $+60$ and $+500$\,mm ---
reproducible across runs, linear at exactly 4.72\,mm per delay tick within each
mode. Same code, same boards, different calling pattern.

We never found the mechanism. Later analysis (\S\ref{sec:phaseA}) showed a small
real timing-state effect exists ($\pm$5--7\,mm), far too small to explain this.
The pragmatic fix: \textbf{calibrate on the exact path production uses}, so
whatever the effect is, it cancels. Recorded honestly as open.

\subsection{Strong signals measure long: the power bias}
\label{sec:powerbias}

With delays calibrated, a clean pattern remained in the data: distance error
correlated with received signal strength at $\rho = +0.78$. Strong signal, reads
long; weak signal, reads short. This is a documented DW1000 characteristic (the
leading-edge detector fires slightly differently with amplitude), and the vendor's
published coarse correction is already applied inside the driver --- what we see is
the residual on top.

We fitted, per anchor $k$, a straight line in the \emph{first-path} power
$p_{\mathrm{fp}}$ (the strength of the earliest signal component --- more stable
than total power):
\begin{equation}
d_{\text{corrected}} \;=\; d_{\text{measured}} \;-\;
\big(a_k\, p_{\mathrm{fp}} + b_k\big),
\qquad a_k \in [7.7,\,9.5]\ \text{mm/dB}.
\end{equation}
The fit lives in \gh{matlab/config/range\_correction.json}{range\_correction.json},
is loaded by \gh{matlab/+dune/loadRangeCorrection.m}{loadRangeCorrection.m}, and is
applied inside \code{solveSweep} with the power clamped to the fitted range so the
line is never extrapolated. Validated leave-one-position-out: fitting on 18 of the
19 campaign positions and testing on the held-out one.

\begin{result}
Floor-wide single-reading accuracy: \textbf{199\,mm $\to$ 94\,mm RMSE}. This one
correction did more than any other single change in the project.
\end{result}

We also tried a neural network and a physics-informed network for this correction.
Both lost to the straight line --- see \S\ref{sec:fail-ml}.

\section{Anchor geometry without the camera: the RF self-survey}
\label{sec:survey}

Everything so far assumed the anchor positions are known --- and until now they came
from the ceiling camera (clicked in, \S\ref{sec:registration}). That is accurate, but
it ties the whole system to a calibrated overhead camera. The self-survey is a
separate feature that answers a different question: \emph{can the anchors work out
their own layout, using only their radios?}

\subsection{The idea}

The anchors can range to \emph{each other}. Ask every pair to do a two-way exchange
and you get the ten pairwise distances of a five-anchor set. A set of pairwise
distances defines a shape --- this is the same fact that lets you reconstruct a map
from a table of city-to-city distances. The mathematical tool is \textbf{classical
multidimensional scaling} (MDS): it turns a distance table into a set of points whose
mutual distances match. No camera is involved at any step.

\begin{plainwords}
Give someone only the distances between five towns and they can redraw the map ---
its shape, orientation and even its mirror-image are free, but the shape is fixed.
The anchors do exactly this for themselves: each measures how far it is from the
others, and the constellation's shape falls out of those numbers alone. The camera
was never in the room.
\end{plainwords}

A tag triggers the survey with a single command; each anchor pair ranges over the air
and the averaged distance is streamed to the host. Crucially the \emph{tag's} own
antenna delay never enters --- it is only the messenger --- so the survey is a clean
look at the anchor constellation. The pieces are
\gh{matlab/scripts/survey\_capture.m}{survey\_capture.m} (trigger and record),
\gh{matlab/scripts/survey\_reconstruct.m}{survey\_reconstruct.m} (MDS and comparison)
and \gh{matlab/scripts/build\_survey\_anchors.m}{build\_survey\_anchors.m} (write an
\code{anchors.json} straight from the survey, camera-free).

\subsection{What it measured}

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.66\textwidth]{figures/self_survey_compare.png}
\caption{Self-survey vs the clicked-Kinect layout. Black squares: the camera's
anchors. Red circles: the survey shape (after a rotation/reflection alignment, since
distances fix no absolute pose). Green crosses: the same after removing each anchor's
antenna-delay bias.}
\label{fig:survey}
\end{figure}

The raw anchor-to-anchor distances read \textbf{long}, by up to 202\,mm (RMS 118\,mm
over the ten pairs), because the anchors' own antenna delays inflate every
measurement --- the exact effect calibrated away in \S\ref{sec:delay}, here seen from
the other side. Reconstructed into a shape and aligned to the camera layout
(rotation and reflection only --- \emph{no scaling}, deliberately, so a global size
error cannot be absorbed), the constellation agrees well: the raw shape lands on the
clicked geometry almost exactly, and after removing a per-anchor delay bias the
residual is \textbf{51\,mm} (Fig.~\ref{fig:survey}).

\begin{result}
The five anchors, using only their own radios, reproduce the camera-measured layout
to within a few centimetres of shape --- and the switch is real: the working
\code{anchors.json} is now built from a self-survey
(layout \code{self\_survey\_...}), not from camera clicks.
\end{result}

\subsection{What it can and cannot prove}

One honest limit, stated because it is easy to oversell. The survey measures
distances that are all biased long by unknown per-anchor delays, and there are five
such unknowns. Those five offsets can partly mimic a genuine change in geometry, so a
\emph{small} residual proves the layout is consistent only \emph{up to} five free
offsets --- necessary, but not sufficient, to declare the geometry perfect. A
\emph{large} residual, by contrast, is unambiguous: it is a real shape disagreement
and would indict the clicked layout. So the self-survey is best read as a powerful
\emph{cross-check} on the camera (and a camera-free fallback when no calibrated
overhead view exists), rather than a strictly better replacement for it. That the two
independent methods agree to centimetres is the reassuring result.

\section{Getting the data to MATLAB}
\label{sec:transport}

Two interchangeable transports feed the host, with the same interface
(\code{start} / \code{drain} / \code{drainEvents} / send):

\begin{itemize}
\item \gh{matlab/+dune/TagSerial.m}{TagSerial.m} --- USB serial, 115200 baud.
      Opening the port resets the tag (DTR line), which is actually useful: you
      always see the boot banner, including which antenna delay is active.
\item \gh{matlab/+dune/TagUdp.m}{TagUdp.m} --- WiFi. The firmware broadcasts every
      sweep to port 4100 already; this class just listens. One socket receives
      \emph{both} tags and the consumer separates them by the tag id in each line.
      It also learns each tag's IP from its traffic, so commands can be sent back.
\end{itemize}

\begin{pitfall}
Two traps here. First, MATLAB's own \code{udpport} needs a toolbox we do not have
--- so \code{TagUdp} uses the Java \code{DatagramSocket} that ships inside MATLAB.
Second, an \emph{empty} Java socket poll costs $\sim$15\,ms (it works by timeout
exception). Harmless in a display loop; catastrophic inside a 20\,Hz control loop,
as \S\ref{sec:fail-loop} recounts. Poll once per loop, not twice.

Also: the first bind pops a Windows Firewall prompt. Decline it and packets vanish
silently forever.
\end{pitfall}

\begin{tryit}
Is the network delivering at all? This prints every arriving line and a per-tag
summary, and is the first thing to run when WiFi ``does not work'':

\end{lstlisting}
For years the project believed the campus WiFi blocked UDP. \code{udp\_probe}

\end{tryit}

\section{The least-squares position solver}
\label{sec:solver}

\subsection{The cost function}

Given corrected distances $d_k$ to anchors at known positions $\mathbf{a}_k$, the
solver (\gh{matlab/+dune/multilaterate.m}{multilaterate.m}) finds the tag position
$\mathbf{p} = (x,y)$ minimising
\begin{equation}
\label{eq:ls}
J(\mathbf{p}) \;=\; \sum_k w_k\,
\Big(\; \underbrace{\lVert \mathbf{p}-\mathbf{a}_k \rVert}_{\text{distance if the tag were at } \mathbf{p}}
\;-\; \underbrace{d_k}_{\text{distance measured}} \;\Big)^2 .
\end{equation}
The tag's height is fixed and known (24\,cm --- it rides a plate, it does not fly),
so only $x$ and $y$ are solved. That makes the problem small, fast, and hard to
break.

\begin{plainwords}
For a candidate position, ask each anchor: ``if the tag were here, how wrong would
your measurement be?'' Square those wrongnesses (so sign does not matter and big
errors hurt disproportionately), weight them by how much you trust each anchor, add
them up. The position with the smallest total wins.
\end{plainwords}

Because distance is a curved function of position there is no closed-form answer.
Levenberg--Marquardt iterates from a starting guess: each step solves
\begin{equation}
\boldsymbol{\delta} = -\big(J^\top W J + \lambda\,\mathrm{diag}(J^\top W J)\big)^{-1}
J^\top W\, \mathbf{r},
\end{equation}
where $\mathbf{r}$ is the vector of per-anchor errors and $J$ its sensitivity to
moving $\mathbf{p}$. The damping $\lambda$ makes it cautious when a step fails and
bold when steps succeed. Warm-starting from the previous position means it
typically converges in two or three iterations.

\subsection{Three defences that matter}

\textbf{Outlier gate.} After a first solve, the spread of the errors is estimated
robustly with the median absolute deviation, $\hat\sigma = 1.4826\cdot
\mathrm{median}|r_k - \tilde r|$ --- a spread estimate that one wild value cannot
inflate. Any measurement further than $3\hat\sigma$ out is dropped and the solve
repeated. A floor of 2\,cm stops the gate from becoming paranoid when all
residuals happen to be tiny.

\textbf{Collinearity guard.} If the anchors that answered lie nearly on a straight
line, the two mirror-image positions across that line fit \emph{equally well} ---
the data genuinely cannot decide. The solver checks the spread of the answering
anchors perpendicular to their principal line (the second singular value of their
centred coordinates) and \textbf{refuses to answer} below 0.3\,m. This is not
cosmetic: when two anchors died mid-run and left three nearly-collinear ones, this
guard was the only honest response (\S\ref{sec:fail-dualtag}).

\textbf{Signal-quality weights} --- covered in full in \S\ref{sec:nlos} below,
because the story of what the NLOS machinery does (and honestly does not) earn
deserves more than a paragraph.

The full per-sweep pipeline --- sentinel rejection, power correction, weights,
solve, diagnostics --- is assembled in
\gh{matlab/+dune/solveSweep.m}{solveSweep.m}, which is the \emph{single} solve path
used by every live tool and every replay. One code path means offline results and
live results can never quietly diverge.

\subsection{Blocked paths: the NLOS machinery in detail}
\label{sec:nlos}

\subsubsection{The physical signature}

When the straight line from tag to anchor is clear (\emph{line of sight}, LOS),
most of the received energy arrives in the first instant --- the direct ray. When
something blocks that line (\emph{non-line-of-sight}, NLOS --- a person, the rover
body, furniture), the direct ray is attenuated but reflections off walls and floor
still arrive, \emph{later}, because their paths are longer. The chip reports both
the total received power $p_{\mathrm{rx}}$ and the power in the earliest-arriving
component $p_{\mathrm{fp}}$, so the situation is visible in one number:
\begin{equation}
g \;=\; p_{\mathrm{rx}} - p_{\mathrm{fp}} \quad [\text{dB}].
\end{equation}

\begin{plainwords}
Think of a shout across a courtyard. In the open, the loudest thing you hear is the
direct shout, and the echoes are quiet afterthoughts: small gap. If a wall blocks
the shouter, all you hear \emph{is} echoes --- lots of total sound, none of it
early: large gap. The gap is a blocked-path detector that needs no extra hardware,
and it is the vendor's own recommended metric (application note APS006). Crucially,
an NLOS distance is not just noisier --- it is \emph{biased long}, because the
energy that does arrive travelled a longer path. That is why a blocked anchor
should count less even before it looks like an outlier.
\end{plainwords}

One same-frame subtlety in the firmware is worth recording: both powers must be
read from the \emph{same received frame}. The anchor's report carries a power
measured on the \emph{other} leg of the exchange; comparing that against the tag's
own first-path reading would make the gap physically meaningless. The tag therefore
overwrites the reported value with its own same-frame measurement (see the
diagnostics block in \gh{libraries/UwbRtls/src/TwrEngine.cpp}{TwrEngine.cpp}).

\subsubsection{From gap to weight}

\gh{matlab/+dune/gapWeights.m}{gapWeights.m} converts the gap into a per-anchor
trust weight:
\begin{equation}
w(g) \;=\;
\max\!\Big(\,0.05,\; 10^{-\max(0,\; g - 3)/10}\,\Big),
\qquad w(\text{no diagnostics}) = 1 .
\end{equation}
In words: up to a 3\,dB gap the anchor is fully trusted ($w{=}1$; small gaps are
normal even in LOS). Beyond that, trust falls by a factor of ten for every 10\,dB
of excess --- mirroring that the gap itself is a logarithmic quantity. The floor of
0.05 means even a badly obstructed anchor is \emph{de-emphasised, never silently
discarded}: keeping a whisper of it preserves solve geometry, and the MAD gate
(\S\ref{sec:solver}) still exists for outright rejection. A missing diagnostic
(sentinel, \S\ref{sec:protocol}) maps to full weight rather than a fabricated
suspicion.

\subsubsection{The three places the weight acts}

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{In the least-squares solve} --- as the $w_k$ of
      Eq.~\eqref{eq:ls}, inside \gh{matlab/+dune/solveSweep.m}{solveSweep.m}.
      A blocked anchor bends the position less.
\item \textbf{In the estimators} --- the same weights ride along as per-range
      measurement trust: the Kalman filter's tightly-coupled range updates divide
      the measurement variance by $w_k$, and both moving-horizon estimators take
      them as $w_{kj}$ in Eq.~\eqref{eq:mhe}, so a suspect range pulls the window
      more gently.
\item \textbf{In the position-fix covariance} --- the loosely-coupled Kalman
      update inflates its measurement noise $R$ by (among other factors) the mean
      gap of the sweep (the \code{adaptiveR} helper in
      \gh{matlab/scripts/live\_tag.m}{live\_tag.m} and
      \gh{matlab/scripts/ekf\_replay.m}{ekf\_replay.m}): a sweep that was
      obstructed overall is trusted less as a whole.
\end{enumerate}

\subsubsection{What the ablation honestly showed}

Good machinery still has to survive being switched off. On 19 parked dwells we ran
the weights off against on: scatter was equal, slow wander was equal, and static
accuracy was actually \emph{slightly better with the weights off} (48\,mm
$\to$ 39\,mm median). We kept them on anyway --- a deliberate judgement, recorded
here so it can be revisited: a parked-room test never exercises the case the
weights exist for, which is \emph{transient} blocking --- a person stepping between
tag and anchor, the rover's own body shadowing a link mid-turn. In those moments
the biased-long range arrives with a large gap attached, and down-weighting it is
exactly right. The static cost of carrying the weights is a few millimetres; the
protection is against decimetre-scale transients.

Two relatives of the idea were tried and dropped. A \emph{hard} drop of
high-gap ranges added nothing over soft weighting in the correction bake-off
(\S\ref{sec:powerbias}) --- unsurprising in hindsight, since the floor-weighted
soft version already contains it. And the chip's separate ``receive quality''
metric turned out to duplicate what power already says (correlation 0.52 with
error, essentially nothing beyond it) and was crossed off.

\subsubsection{And the CIR? Diagnosis only --- so far}

The channel impulse response of \S\ref{sec:breathing} is the \emph{full} version
of the story the gap summarises in one number: energy per $\approx$30\,cm of path,
tap by tap. It would be natural to ask whether it feeds the pipeline. It does not
--- its one use was diagnostic, and it was decisive there (it settled the parked
drift's root cause). Two designed-but-deferred uses sit in the plan: streaming a
short CIR tail with every exchange to fit a \emph{per-reading error model} (predict
each range's error from the echo shape, rather than from power alone), and
revisiting the learned power correction of \S\ref{sec:fail-ml} with CIR features
once dense moving truth exists. The CIR-as-input idea is arguably the most
promising unexploited item in the backlog.

\section{The Kalman filter}
\label{sec:ekf}

A single reading is noisy; the rover moves smoothly. A Kalman filter
(\gh{matlab/+dune/FusionEkf.m}{FusionEkf.m}) exploits that by carrying a
\emph{belief} --- position, velocity, and how uncertain both are --- forward
through time, correcting it with each new fix.

\textbf{Predict.} Between readings the state
$\mathbf{x} = [p_x, p_y, v_x, v_y]^\top$ advances under constant velocity,
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p} \leftarrow \mathbf{p} + \mathbf{v}\,\Delta t,
\qquad
\mathbf{v} \leftarrow \mathbf{v} + \text{(unknown acceleration)},
\end{equation}
and the uncertainty grows by how much acceleration we are willing to imagine
($\sigma_a = 0.8$\,m/s$^2$ while moving). This knob is the responsiveness/smoothness
trade: too big and the filter chases noise, too small and it lags corners.

\textbf{Update.} A new fix $\mathbf{z}$ is compared with the prediction; the
surprise $\mathbf{y} = \mathbf{z} - \hat{\mathbf{p}}$ is weighed against the
combined uncertainty $S$, and the state moves part-way toward the measurement:
$\mathbf{x} \leftarrow \mathbf{x} + K\mathbf{y}$ with gain
$K = P H^\top S^{-1}$. The scalar $\mathbf{y}^\top S^{-1}\mathbf{y}$ (``how many
sigmas of surprise'') is $\chi^2$-gated at 95\,\%.

Five additions, each earned by an observed failure:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Robust updates, not hard rejection.} A fix slightly beyond the gate
      is not discarded; its assumed noise is inflated so its surprise sits exactly
      at the gate --- it still pulls, just gently (the Huber idea, borrowed from
      the Bitcraze flight stack). Only fixes beyond $10\times$ the gate are
      rejected outright. One failing mode improved 164\,mm~$\to$~105\,mm from this
      change alone.
\item \textbf{ZUPT.} When the IMU's recent window is quiet
      ($\max|\mathbf{a}| < 0.12$\,m/s$^2$, $\max|\boldsymbol\omega| < 0.05$\,rad/s
      over 8 samples), feed a pseudo-measurement $\mathbf{v} = 0$. The window
      matters: instantaneous thresholds fire mid-stride.
\item \textbf{stillMode.} ZUPT alone did not pin a parked tag --- the process
      noise kept re-opening the gain ($\sim$30\,mm of fresh position uncertainty
      per step). When still, $\sigma_a$ drops to 0.05\,m/s$^2$ and the dot
      genuinely settles.
\item \textbf{Per-anchor bias states.} Each anchor's residual bias is learned as an
      extra state, $d_k = \lVert\mathbf{p}-\mathbf{a}_k\rVert + b_k$, a slow random
      walk that is \emph{remembered while the anchor is absent}. Why: when the
      answering subset changes, the average bias changes, and the position used to
      jump 13--30\,mm. With bias memory: \textbf{6--9\,mm}.
\item \textbf{RangeHold} (\gh{matlab/+dune/RangeHold.m}{RangeHold.m}). The
      complementary trick at the measurement level: an anchor that misses a sweep
      contributes its recent median for a short grace period, so the solve
      geometry never collapses. Subset jumps at the raw level: 13--28\,mm $\to$
      \textbf{6--10\,mm}.
\end{enumerate}

Everything above can be re-run offline against any logged session:
\begin{lstlisting}

% with a static campaign for scoring against truth:
ekf_replay(srcLog, spotsJson="matlab/results/static_accuracy_.../spots.json")
\end{lstlisting}
\end{tryit}

\section{The slow ``breathing'', and how we hunted it}
\label{sec:breathing}

With all of the above, a parked tag still wandered \textbf{5--7\,cm over seconds to
minutes} --- slow, smooth, and indistinguishable from genuine creeping motion. This
section matters beyond this project: it is a worked example of isolating an error
you cannot remove.

\subsection{Phase A: interrogating existing logs}
\label{sec:phaseA}

Three hypotheses were tested purely on recorded data
(\gh{matlab/scripts/phase\_a\_forensics.m}{phase\_a\_forensics.m}, findings in
\gh{docs/phase\_a\_findings.md}{phase\_a\_findings.md}).

\textbf{A1 --- is it measurement timing?} The anomaly of \S\ref{sec:anomaly}
suggested distance depends on exchange cadence, and production cadence wanders
(retries, skips). Across 44\,000 measurements, the correlation between each
anchor's realised timing gap and its distance error was $|\rho| \le 0.06$.
\emph{Rejected} --- though a genuine micro-effect exists: a measurement following
extra receiver idle time shifts by $\pm$5--7\,mm, and the first reading after a
long gap reads $+39$\,mm. Two orders of magnitude too small.

\textbf{A2 --- is it the tag or the links?} The decisive split. If the tag's
clock/temperature drifted, \emph{all five} distances would breathe together
(common mode). If the radio paths are each doing their own thing, they breathe
independently. Measured cross-anchor correlation of the slow residuals over 23
parked dwells: \textbf{+0.05} --- independent. One number eliminated the tag's
clock, temperature and battery, and pointed at the individual links.

\textbf{A3 --- can the filter absorb it?} We tried inverting the noise budget while
parked: trust position completely, let the per-anchor bias states soak up the
drift. It made things dramatically worse (91\,mm $\to$ 319\,mm) --- see
\S\ref{sec:fail-inversion} for the instructive reason.

\subsection{Phase B: looking at the radio waveform itself}

The DW1000 can dump its channel impulse response (CIR) --- the received signal
amplitude tap by tap, one tap $\approx$ 30\,cm of path. We added a capture command
to the firmware (\code{CIR,<anchor>} in
\gh{libraries/UwbRtls/src/TwrEngine.cpp}{TwrEngine.cpp}) and a waterfall tool
(\gh{matlab/scripts/cir\_capture.m}{cir\_capture.m}).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=0.8\textwidth]{figures/cir_waterfall_A4.png}
\caption{Twenty-eight consecutive channel snapshots to anchor A4, aligned on the
detected first path (red line). The energy \emph{at} the first path is faint; the
bright band arriving $\sim$25 taps ($\approx$7.5\,m of extra path) later is a
reflection much stronger than the direct signal.}
\end{figure}

The picture was unambiguous. The direct path --- the one whose arrival time
\emph{is} the distance --- sits roughly \textbf{20\,dB below the strongest
reflection} and only 8--10\,dB above the noise floor. With antennas 24\,cm off the
floor, the direct ray grazes the ground and its floor reflection arrives almost on
top of it; the detector locks onto a blend, and as the blend shifts, centimetres of
apparent distance come and go. A quiet empty room breathed exactly as much as one
with a person walking (132\,mm peak-to-peak over 81\,s either way): this is the
geometry, not passers-by.

\begin{result}
The breathing is per-link multipath caused by low antenna mounting. The genuine fix
is physical --- raise the antennas (or the tags). The anchors are permanently
installed, so this is deferred, and the system is honest about carrying a 5--7\,cm
slow parked drift.
\end{result}

\subsection{Living with it: the output pin and smoother}
\label{sec:pin}

A parked rover \emph{is} stationary --- we know it from the IMU and from the solver
itself. So at the \textbf{display/logging layer only}, when the system is confident
it is parked, the reported pose freezes: movement within a 5\,cm deadband is
ignored, a sustained excursion ($\sim$1.5\,s beyond the band) re-pins, and any real
motion releases instantly. While moving, a light exponential smoother
($\alpha = 0.6$) takes the high-frequency jitter off, chosen from measurement:
it removed $\sim$50\,\% of jerk for 25\,mm of lag, whereas tuning the filter's
process noise managed only 13\,\% for triple the lag (\S\ref{sec:fail-smoothing}).

This is deliberately cosmetic. The estimator state is untouched (A3 showed why
touching it goes wrong), the raw estimate is what gets logged, and the title bar
says \code{PIN} whenever the freeze is active. Implementation: the pin/smoother
block in \gh{matlab/scripts/live\_tag.m}{live\_tag.m}.

\section{The moving-horizon estimator}
\label{sec:mhe}

\subsection{Why look at more than one reading}

A Kalman filter digests one reading and moves on; it can never revisit a decision.
A \textbf{moving-horizon estimator} (MHE) keeps the last $N$ readings (here
$N{=}10$, about two seconds) and, at every step, re-solves the \emph{entire recent
trajectory}. New evidence gets to reinterpret the recent past. The cost, solved
over all window states $\{\mathbf{x}_k\}$ at once:

\begin{equation}
\label{eq:mhe}
\min_{\{\mathbf{x}_k\}}\;
\underbrace{\sum_{k}\sum_{j} w_{kj}\,
\rho\big(\lVert\mathbf{p}_k-\mathbf{a}_j\rVert - d_{kj}\big)}_{\text{fit every distance in the window}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\sum_{k}\big\lVert \mathbf{x}_{k+1}-f(\mathbf{x}_k)\big\rVert^2_{Q^{-1}}}_{\text{move like a real vehicle}}
\;+\;
\underbrace{\big\lVert \mathbf{x}_1-\bar{\mathbf{x}}\big\rVert^2_{P^{-1}}}_{\text{respect what came before}}
\end{equation}

The robust loss is the pseudo-Huber function
\begin{equation}
\rho(r) = \delta^2\Big(\sqrt{1+(r/\delta)^2}-1\Big),
\qquad \delta = 0.15\ \text{m},
\end{equation}
which behaves like $r^2/2$ for small errors but grows only \emph{linearly} for
large ones --- a wild measurement bends the answer instead of dragging it.
The ``arrival cost'' (third term) is how information older than the window
survives: the window's first state is softly tied to what the previous solve
believed.

Implemented in \gh{matlab/+dune/MheEstimator.m}{MheEstimator.m} using
CasADi~3.7.0 (symbolic derivatives) and IPOPT (the solver). Median solve time:
\textbf{3\,ms} --- easily real-time at 6\,Hz.

\begin{result}
Same recorded data, MHE vs Kalman: while \emph{moving}, the MHE track is
\textbf{40--44\,\% smoother} (jerk 0.36 vs 0.61) for 6\,mm extra lag. Parked, a
tie (61 vs 64\,mm RMSE). It is offered as an opt-in
(\code{live\_tag(..., estimator="mhe")}), not the default, because at 2 of 19
parked spots the breathing slowly drags the whole window $\sim$0.23\,m --- the
average stays right, but the drift is real.
\end{result}

\subsection{The jump guard}

IPOPT occasionally lands on a different-but-similar optimum between consecutive
solves, making the output leap. The guard: if the new answer jumps further from
the constant-velocity prediction than 0.20\,m (or the solver reports
non-convergence), discard it, fall back to the \emph{measurement-only} fix, and
re-seed the window. The first version of this guard fell back to dead reckoning
instead and diverged nine metres --- the full story is \S\ref{sec:fail-guard},
and the design rule it taught is printed there in bold.

\subsection{Motion models: using the IMU without trusting it}
\label{sec:unicycle}

We probed what the tag's IMU can honestly provide (log-based, in
\S\ref{sec:fail-imuaccel}): its accelerometer bias is $\approx$0, its \emph{turn
rate} is trustworthy, but its absolute compass direction is rotated by an unknown
amount. The safe pattern: use \textbf{changes}, never absolutes.

\begin{itemize}
\item \textbf{cv} --- constant velocity, gyro optional: the velocity vector is
      rotated by the measured yaw rate $\omega\,\Delta t$ each step. With
      $\omega = 0$ it reduces exactly to plain constant velocity, so it can never
      be worse.
\item \textbf{unicycle} --- for a car-like vehicle. State
      $[p_x, p_y, \psi, s]$: heading and forward speed. The gyro drives the
      heading ($\psi_{k+1} = \psi_k + \omega\,\Delta t$, softly), and velocity is
      \emph{defined} as $s\,[\cos\psi, \sin\psi]$ --- sideways motion is not
      representable, because a front-steering car cannot slide sideways. The UWB
      measurements anchor the absolute heading that the gyro alone cannot know.
\end{itemize}

\section{The rigid-body estimator}
\label{sec:rigid}

\subsection{One body, not two dots}

The two tags are bolted to one plate, $L = 0.527$\,m apart (measured antenna
centre to antenna centre --- it matters, this number becomes exact in the math).
Estimating them separately throws that knowledge away. Instead,
\gh{matlab/+dune/MheRigid.m}{MheRigid.m} estimates a single body state
$[c_x, c_y, \psi, s]$ --- centre, heading, speed --- and \emph{derives} both tags
from it:
\begin{equation}
\mathbf{p}_{\text{front}} = \mathbf{c} + \tfrac{L}{2}
\begin{bmatrix}\cos\psi\\ \sin\psi\end{bmatrix},
\qquad
\mathbf{p}_{\text{rear}} = \mathbf{c} - \tfrac{L}{2}
\begin{bmatrix}\cos\psi\\ \sin\psi\end{bmatrix}.
\end{equation}

\begin{plainwords}
This is constraint by \emph{construction}, not by penalty --- and the distinction
is the whole design. A penalty says ``please keep the tags 52.7\,cm apart'' and can
be outvoted by noisy data. This parameterisation \emph{cannot express} any other
separation: there is no combination of $c$, $\psi$, $s$ that puts the tags at the
wrong distance. Three facts come for free: the separation is exact in every
answer; ``both stopped or both moving'' is automatic (one speed state); and the
heading is observed by the radios (which end is where) \emph{and} steered by the
gyro --- better than either source alone.
\end{plainwords}

The two tags report at different instants (the token ring alternates), so there is
no pairing of readings in time. Each arriving sweep --- from either tag --- becomes
its own node in the horizon, labelled $+1$ (front) or $-1$ (rear), observing the
body at its own timestamp. Terrain is handled with the IMU's roll and pitch taken
\emph{directly} (they are gravity-referenced and excellent, \S\ref{sec:sixdof}):
on a pitched slope the horizontal tag separation shrinks to $L\cos\theta$ and the
two antennas sit at heights $z_0 \pm \tfrac{L}{2}\sin\theta$ --- both effects are
in the measurement model.

Two parameters keep the body speed physical (\code{speedMax},
\code{allowReverse}). Without a bound the optimiser can explain one noisy sweep with
an unphysical speed spike that unwinds on the next solve --- a jitter source seen as
a non-zero fused speed while the rig is parked --- so the speed is bounded to a small
margin above the commanded maximum. The reverse option matters more subtly: the
unicycle description is sign-ambiguous, since speed $+v$ at heading $\psi$ and speed
$-v$ at heading $\psi+180^\circ$ are the same motion. Near standstill the gyro barely
breaks that tie, so the estimate can flip between the two (heading snaps
$180^\circ$, speed flickers sign). Forbidding reverse removes one whole branch and
kills the flip; it is opt-in, enabled only when the vehicle genuinely never backs up
(the model itself defaults to the general symmetric bound).

How good can the heading be? With per-distance noise $\sigma_d$ and baseline $L$,
the geometric limit per reading is roughly
\begin{equation}
\sigma_\psi \approx \frac{\sqrt{2}\,\sigma_d}{L}
\;\;\xrightarrow{\;\sigma_d = 30\,\text{mm},\ L = 0.527\,\text{m}\;}\;
\approx 4.6^\circ\ \text{per reading},
\end{equation}
which filtering and the gyro then improve. A longer baseline buys heading accuracy
linearly --- worth remembering if the plate is ever redesigned.

\section{Driving the rover: truth and radio on one clock}
\label{sec:rover}

The final piece closes the loop with ground truth \emph{in motion}. The rover
already carries an AprilTag that the calibrated Kinect tracks; driving it with both
UWB tags aboard records, simultaneously and on one clock: where it truly went, and
what the radios said.

\gh{matlab/scripts/rover\_teleop\_uwb.m}{rover\_teleop\_uwb.m} was ported from the
existing MMS rover teleoperation script (used read-only --- the MMS codebase is not
modified). It drives the rover with a PS4 controller at 20\,Hz while logging:
commanded velocities, AprilTag pose, the rover's own IMU and wheel encoders, and
every UWB sweep from both tags, all POSIX-timestamped.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{PS4 controls (unchanged from the original teleop).}
\small
\begin{tabular}{@{}ll@{}}
\toprule
D-pad up/down & linear speed $\pm$0.01\,m/s (max 0.10)\\
D-pad left/right & turn rate $\pm$0.01\,rad/s (max 0.107)\\

One safety decision is worth its own paragraph: \textbf{the estimator does not run
inside the driving loop.} That loop services the emergency stop; the MHE
occasionally takes 90\,ms; delaying an E-stop to compute a position is a bad trade
in every universe. The loop only \emph{records} (microseconds); estimation happens
offline on the recording and is bit-identical, since the sweeps are all the
estimator consumes.

\subsection{Scoring a run}

\gh{matlab/scripts/rover\_run\_report.m}{rover\_run\_report.m} turns a run into
numbers. Two frame subtleties are handled explicitly rather than assumed away:

\emph{The lever arm.} The AprilTag is not at the rig centre --- it sits 11.35\,cm
behind and 10\,cm to the right of it (measured). That offset \emph{rotates with
the rover}, so truth is de-rotated per sample:
$\mathbf{c}_{\text{true}} = \mathbf{p}_{\text{tag}} -
R(\psi)\,\boldsymbol{\ell}$, with $\boldsymbol{\ell} = [-0.1135, -0.10]$\,m.

\emph{The frame fit.} The camera world and the UWB anchor world are two different
coordinate systems. The best rigid mapping (rotation $+$ translation, no scale)
between the two trajectories is found by the classical SVD construction: centre
both point sets, form the cross-covariance $H = \sum_i \tilde{\mathbf{u}}_i
\tilde{\mathbf{t}}_i^\top$, decompose $H = U\Sigma V^\top$, and take
$R = V\,\mathrm{diag}(1, \det(VU^\top))\,U^\top$. Fitting this \emph{from data}
(rather than assuming the frames coincide) also acts as a cross-check: the
rotation it finds should be stable run to run.

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/rover_run_trajectory.png}
\caption{First driven run (212\,s). Top-left: raw fixes and the rigid-MHE track in
the UWB frame. Top-middle: AprilTag truth. Top-right: the two overlaid after the
fitted frame transform --- the shapes agree. Bottom row: commanded velocities, UWB
per-sweep health, truth availability with error over time.}
\label{fig:roverrun}
\end{figure}

\begin{result}
First run: median error \textbf{98\,mm}, RMSE 268\,mm, frame fit $-3.6^\circ$ /
$[-0.46, -0.59]$\,m. The RMSE is stated but \textbf{not yet trusted}, and
Figure~\ref{fig:roverrun} shows exactly why: the camera saw the marker only
58\,\% of the time with one long gap (the biggest ``errors'' are unmeasured
stretches), the rear tag was starved (386 vs 1124 sweeps --- weakly constrained
heading, 46\,\% guard trips), and only 3.6 of 5 anchors answered on average.
These are setup problems, not algorithm problems, and they are the current work.
\end{result}

\section{The full six degrees of freedom}
\label{sec:sixdof}

The rover state we ultimately want is $x, y, z$ and roll, pitch, yaw.
\gh{matlab/scripts/rover\_pose\_report.m}{rover\_pose\_report.m} compares every
channel we can observe, from every sensor that observes it
(Figure~\ref{fig:sixdof}).

\begin{figure}[h]
\centering
\includegraphics[width=\textwidth]{figures/rover_run_6dof.png}
\caption{All six channels from the first run. Blue is the AprilTag; red/green are
the two IMUs; magenta is the UWB rigid-MHE yaw. Note pitch and roll: the camera
(blue) swings tens of degrees while the two independent IMUs agree with each other
and stay flat --- the camera is the outlier there.}
\label{fig:sixdof}
\end{figure}

\begin{pitfall}
Two traps found while making this plot. The AprilTag angles are in
\emph{radians} but the rover's onboard IMU reports \emph{degrees} --- verified
from the data ranges, not assumed. And every sensor defines ``zero heading''
differently (marker print orientation, IMU mounting, anchor frame), so channels
are compared after removing a constant offset (a circular mean); the offsets
themselves are reported and should stay stable run to run.
\end{pitfall}

The comparison produced the most useful architectural fact of the rover phase:
\textbf{the camera is only trustworthy in-plane.} Its $z$ wanders with 157\,mm
standard deviation over a flat floor; its pitch and roll swing by tens of degrees
while the two \emph{independent} gravity-referenced IMUs agree with each other to
a few degrees. A small, flat, distant marker simply does not constrain
out-of-plane pose well --- a known weakness, now measured in our own data. The
IMUs win those channels: gravity is an absolute reference.

\begin{table}[h]
\centering
\caption{Who to believe, per channel --- the sensors are complementary.}
\begin{tabular}{@{}llll@{}}
\toprule
\textbf{Channel} & \textbf{Best source} & \textbf{Quality} & \textbf{Truth for scoring}\\
\midrule
$x, y$      & UWB rigid MHE & 98\,mm median (first run) & AprilTag (in-plane: valid)\\
yaw         & UWB rigid MHE & \textbf{7.3$^\circ$ RMS}  & AprilTag (in-plane: valid)\\

Two notes on that table. The UWB yaw agreeing with camera yaw at 7.3$^\circ$ RMS
--- with a residual frame offset of only 1.4$^\circ$ after a transform fitted from
\emph{position} data alone --- is a strong consistency check: heading and position
tell the same story. And $z$ is not a software gap: all five anchors sit at the
same height, so the radio geometry contains almost no vertical information. If $z$
ever matters, anchors must move to different heights; no algorithm can conjure
observability that the geometry does not contain.

\section{What we tried that did not work}
\label{sec:failures}

Kept deliberately, at full length. Several of these consumed more effort than the
things that worked, and each ends with the rule it taught.

\subsection{Predicting motion from the accelerometer}
\label{sec:fail-imuaccel}
Feeding the IMU's acceleration into the filter's prediction diverged by 6--14\,m.
For a long time we blamed accelerometer bias --- the classic culprit. Later
measurement showed the bias is essentially zero
($[-0.007, -0.026, +0.013]$\,m/s$^2$ at rest); the real fault is that the IMU's
absolute orientation frame is \emph{rotated} by an unknown amount, so perfectly
good accelerations were applied in the wrong direction, and the gate then locked
out the corrections that would have caught it. Gyro turn \emph{rate} needs no
absolute frame and works fine. \textbf{Rule: diagnose which property is broken
(bias vs frame) before designing the fix --- and prefer differential quantities
when an absolute reference is unverified.}

\subsection{Feeding raw distances into the filter}
Updating the Kalman filter with each distance separately (tightly-coupled) is
textbook-better: it works with fewer than three anchors. On real data it scored
2244\,mm against 335\,mm loosely-coupled. With per-distance gating, the filter can
sit at a wrong position that keeps one or two distances happy and reject the rest
forever --- self-consistent and wrong. Robust updates later tamed it (105\,mm) but
never beat the simple version. \textbf{Rule: a partial-acceptance mechanism needs a
global sanity anchor, or it will find a lie it likes.}

\subsection{Machine learning for the power correction}
\label{sec:fail-ml}
The straight-line power correction (\S\ref{sec:powerbias}) was challenged with an
MLP and a physics-informed network with a monotonicity prior. Scored honestly ---
leave-one-\emph{position}-out --- the line won: 98\,mm vs 107 vs 108. There were
31\,500 samples but only 19 independent positions; the effective sample size was
19, and extra capacity fitted noise. \textbf{Rule: count independent conditions,
not rows; and make the baseline a straight line before reaching for a network.}

\subsection{Absorbing the parked drift into the filter}
\label{sec:fail-inversion}
While parked, why not trust position fully and let the per-anchor bias states soak
up the breathing? Because the biases are \emph{spot-specific}: values learned at
one parking spot are wrong at the next, and the whole point of bias states is that
they are remembered. The error compounded across dwells: 91\,mm $\to$ 319\,mm.
\textbf{Rule: never let a long-memory state absorb a location-dependent error.}

\subsection{The guard that dead-reckoned into the wall}
\label{sec:fail-guard}
The MHE jump guard originally fell back to the constant-velocity prediction. Once
one bad solve slipped through, the prediction walked; every subsequent honest
answer now looked like a ``jump'' relative to it, so the guard kept rejecting
reality --- a self-reinforcing runaway that ended nine metres off, with the guard
firing on 73\,\% of solves. Falling back to the measurement-only fix instead ended
the class of failure (3\,\% trips). \textbf{Rule: a fallback must be anchored to
measurements; a fallback anchored to prediction can chase its own tail.}

\subsection{Two independent tag estimates}
\label{sec:fail-dualtag}
The first dual-tag design ran two separate estimators and drew both. In one live
session the two dots slid $\sim$3.5\,m apart while the physical tags sat 30\,cm
apart. Two stacked causes: two anchors' batteries died mid-run, leaving three
nearly-collinear anchors --- so the solver \emph{correctly} refused to answer
(mirror ambiguity, \S\ref{sec:solver}); and with no fixes arriving, the display
followed each filter's dead-reckoned prediction instead of freezing. The redesign
went to the single rigid pose (\S\ref{sec:rigid}) --- which \emph{cannot express}
the tags drifting apart --- plus a display that freezes and says
\code{COASTING}, plus per-anchor health markers so a dying anchor is seen in
seconds. \textbf{Rule: make impossible states unrepresentable, and never display a
prediction as if it were a measurement.}

\subsection{Tuning the motion model for smoothness}
\label{sec:fail-smoothing}
The obvious smoothness knob --- filter process noise --- was swept from 0.8 down
to 0.1\,m/s$^2$: only 13\,\% less jerk for $3\times$ the lag. The roughness lives
at the same timescale as genuine manoeuvres, so the model cannot separate them; a
light \emph{output} smoother achieved 50\,\% for 25\,mm of lag.
\textbf{Rule: identify which layer owns an error before tuning; cosmetic errors
get cosmetic fixes.}

\subsection{The gyro turn model that showed nothing --- yet}
On hand-carried test data, adding the gyro coordinated-turn made no measurable
difference (jerk 0.357 vs 0.362). The data contained no sustained turns to
exploit --- a hand slides; it does not arc like a car. Unvalidated is not
disproven; the rover's arcs are where it should pay. \textbf{Rule: a negative
result only counts if the data actually exercises the mechanism.}

\subsection{Correcting a lens that needed none}
Fitting distortion to a lens with $<1.5$\,px of bow produced garbage parameters
from click noise (\S\ref{sec:camera}). \textbf{Rule: measure the size of an effect
before modelling it.}

\subsection{Calibration that survived reflashing}
The NVS trap of \S\ref{sec:delay}: stored calibration outliving firmware, causing
metre-scale ``bugs''. \textbf{Rule: wipe persistent storage on bring-up, and make
devices print their active calibration at boot.}

\subsection{The 20\,Hz loop that became 1.4\,Hz}
\label{sec:fail-loop}
When UWB recording was added to the rover teleop, the PS4 stopped ``working''.
The controller was fine --- the loop had silently slowed to 1.4\,Hz, and a 200\,ms
button press fits entirely between two 700\,ms polls. Three self-inflicted costs:
the big recording arrays were placed \emph{inside} the struct that a logging
function takes by value (MATLAB copy-on-write duplicated $\sim$13\,MB per
iteration); the UDP socket was polled twice per loop at $\sim$15\,ms per empty
poll (Java timeout exception); and a timezone-aware timestamp call cost 0.64\,ms
each. All were found by measurement, not inspection
(\S the benchmark lives in the commit message of \code{2703da1}). The loop now
prints its own achieved rate and warns below 8\,Hz.
\textbf{Rule: real-time loops get instrumented, always --- a slow loop looks
exactly like broken hardware.}

\subsection{Deferred, not failed}
Two designed-but-parked items, both aimed at the breathing's root cause: raising
the tags/antennas out of the ground-grazing zone (the anchors cannot move; a mast
for the tags is the practical variant), and a broadcast ranging protocol (one
poll, all anchors answer in slots --- all boards need reflashing). And one
firmware fix sits staged but unflashed: the diagnostic temperature/CFO reads
currently return zeros due to two read-timing bugs, found and fixed in commit
\code{ac5743c}.

\section{Where things stand, and the road ahead}
\label{sec:roadmap}

Built and working end to end: calibrated ranging, robust solving, two filters, a
rigid two-tag pose with heading, wireless transport, live displays with honest
failure behaviour, a teleoperated rover that records truth and radio together, and
offline scoring. The build phase is closed; the work now is
\textbf{testing and tuning}:

\begin{enumerate}
\item \textbf{Data quality of truth runs} --- keep the AprilTag in the camera's
      view (58\,\% coverage made the first run's RMSE meaningless); find why tag
      240 was starved of sweeps; keep all five anchors alive (fresh batteries,
      watch the health markers).
\item \textbf{Score the estimator family} on clean runs: Kalman vs MHE-cv vs
      MHE-unicycle vs rigid --- same logs, same truth, one table.
\item \textbf{Validate the gyro-turn and no-slip models} on real arcs, checking
      the gyro sign convention on the vehicle.
\item \textbf{If parked drift must shrink}: the physical fixes --- tag mast
      first, then the deferred diagnostics (locked-room dwell with the repaired
      temperature/CFO telemetry).
\end{enumerate}

The honest closing numbers: \textbf{94\,mm} for one raw reading parked;
\textbf{60--65\,mm} filtered; \textbf{7.3$^\circ$} heading against truth;
\textbf{14\,mm / 1.2$^\circ$} for the rigid estimator when the data is clean
(synthetic); and a moving-accuracy number that awaits a clean run to be worth
printing.

\section{Glossary}
\label{app:glossary}

\begin{description}[style=nextline]
\item[Anchor / tag] Fixed radio at a known position / mobile radio being located.
\item[DS-TWR] Double-sided two-way ranging; the four-timestamp exchange of
  \S\ref{sec:twr} that cancels clock-rate error.
\item[Antenna delay] Per-board constant for time spent inside the electronics;
  1 tick $=$ 4.69\,mm.
\item[First-path power] Strength of the earliest-arriving signal component ---
  the direct ray, ideally.
\item[NLOS] Non-line-of-sight: the direct path is blocked; energy arrives late
  via reflections.
\item[CIR] Channel impulse response --- the received energy tap by tap
  ($\approx$30\,cm per tap); the radio's own picture of the room's echoes.
\item[ZUPT] Zero-velocity update: telling the filter ``it is stationary'' as a
  measurement.
\item[NIS / $\chi^2$ gate] ``How many sigmas of surprise'' a measurement carries,
  and the acceptance threshold on it.
\item[MHE] Moving-horizon estimation: re-solving the recent trajectory over a
  sliding window (\S\ref{sec:mhe}).
\item[Arrival cost] The MHE term that ties the window's start to prior belief, so
  old information is not forgotten.
\item[Unicycle model] Vehicle model with heading + forward speed and no sideways
  motion --- a front-steering car.
\item[Breathing] Our name for the slow (5--7\,cm, seconds-to-minutes) parked
  drift caused by ground-grazing multipath (\S\ref{sec:breathing}).
\item[Lever arm] The fixed offset between a sensor and the reference point it is
  supposed to measure; rotates with the body.
\end{description}
